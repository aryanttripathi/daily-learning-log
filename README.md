# Daily Learning Log

A daily technical learning log that goes deep. Each entry targets the actual mechanics of a system (storage layouts, wire protocols, scheduler behaviour, failure modes) rather than a feature tour or a summary of marketing pages.

Every lesson is written to be *runnable*: it carries a hands-on exercise you can execute against a real system, at least one structural diagram of the mechanism under discussion, and a source list containing only links that were actually read while writing it.

## How This Works

- **One subject at a time.** The log follows a multi-week *track*: a sequential deep dive into a single database or systems subject. The track's syllabus and progress live in [`TRACK.md`](TRACK.md), which is the single source of truth for what has been taught and what comes next.
- **One lesson per day, each building on the last.** Every day takes the first unchecked lesson from the syllabus. Lessons assume everything earlier in the track is known and open with a *Where This Fits* section linking the previous lesson. Missed days are never backfilled; the track simply continues.
- **Scope is handed forward explicitly.** A comment block in `TRACK.md` records, per lesson, what it took, what it deliberately left for a later lesson, which source commit it read, which measurements reproduced, and which did not. That is what keeps a 33-lesson sequence from re-teaching itself or quietly contradicting an earlier day. `TRACK.md` keeps the notes for the three most recent lessons; older ones roll off into git history, and [`TRACK-ARCHIVE.md`](TRACK-ARCHIVE.md) records the exact commit and blob to read for each lesson range. Nothing is deleted and nothing is duplicated — the lesson documents themselves are the real record.
- **A separate Daily Diff digest every day.** Alongside the lesson, each day folder has a `daily-diff.md` covering an edition of [The Daily Diff](https://tdd.cat/): a point-wise summary plus a deep dive on the single most significant item, with its source link followed and the underlying thing read. tdd.cat publishes editions on a documented two-day settling window, and **its endpoints are independently cached, so any one of them can be a day behind or fail outright.** The front page, [`/md`](https://tdd.cat/md) (the documented "latest edition" endpoint), the dated `https://tdd.cat/{YYYY-MM-DD}.md` permalink, the dated `https://tdd.cat/{YYYY-MM-DD}/` page and [`/archive/`](https://tdd.cat/archive/) have each been observed leading, lagging or failing while the others worked, in a **different pattern on each of four consecutive days**: on 2026-10-11 the dated pages would not fetch at all and the front page was stale; on 2026-10-12 the dated page was the *only* one with the newest edition and `/md` was stale; on 2026-10-13 the front page and `/archive/` agreed and were current, `.md` worked, and the dated trailing-slash page was the one that failed; on 2026-10-14 the dated trailing-slash page failed again while the dated `.md` and `/md` both worked **and agreed on the item count**, with the front page a day behind. So the procedure is: take the newest date from [`/archive/`](https://tdd.cat/archive/), then try **both** `https://tdd.cat/{date}/` and `https://tdd.cat/{date}.md`, use whichever answers, and cross-check the item count against any other endpoint that answers. ([`llms.txt`](https://tdd.cat/llms.txt) is the source for the endpoint list.) A digest covers the newest edition not yet covered, says which one it is, and records how it was found. When no new edition has published, the digest says so explicitly rather than inventing items. Editions are identified by **date**, never by the archive's edition number, which has been observed to move *backwards* between consecutive days (079 for 2026-10-11, then 055 for 2026-10-12); item counts vary per edition and vary *wildly* — 30, 26, 18, 21 and then **3** on five consecutive days, which is why the count is always cross-checked against a second endpoint before a short edition is reported as short.
- **Tracks end with a capstone.** When every lesson is checked, that day's document is a capstone assembling the whole system end to end. The subject moves to *Completed Subjects* in `TRACK.md`, and a new track begins.

**SQLite Internals**: Lesson 29 of 33 · started 2026-09-16 · [syllabus and progress →](TRACK.md)

Part I (the on-disk format and the b-tree layer), Part II (the pager, journals, and the OS interface), Part III (write-ahead logging in depth) and Part IV (the SQL compiler and bytecode engine) are complete. **Part V — operational and advanced topics — is now open.** Lesson 21 covered the front end (`tokenize.c`'s 256-byte `aiClass[]` table, the keyword hash that packs 147 keywords into a measured 666 bytes, and Lemon's `%fallback`); lesson 22 covered name resolution (`resolve.c`'s outward `NameContext` walk, the seven candidate sources `lookupName()` tries in order, and a reproducible wrong answer on 3.45.1); lesson 23 covered the VDBE itself (the single allocation holding program, registers and cursors; the bump-plus-eight-slot register allocator; the `Mem` flag word caching two types at once); lesson 24 covered the type system around that flag word (affinity as seven consecutive ASCII characters riding in `OP_Eq.p5`, and a `UNIQUE … COLLATE NOCASE` index that rejects two genuinely distinct keys); lesson 25 covered the code that emits all of it; lesson 26 covered the decision layer (`WhereLoop` as an *algorithm* carrying `(rSetup, rRun, nOut)`, the five closed-form cost formulas, the tie rule that hands equal costs to the full table scan, and the `SEARCH`/`SCAN` crossover predicted in closed form and matched to the single row at four table sizes); lesson 27 covered the statistics those costs are computed from (`sqlite_stat1`, `sqlite_stat4`'s 24 samples chosen partly by a deterministic LCG, `aAvgEq` derived to the unit, and `PRAGMA analysis_limit=100` writing `101` where the truth was `19048`); lesson 28 covered the VDBE sorter (the PMA on-disk format decoded byte by byte, the 16-PMA cliff reachable by *one page* of `cache_size` that exactly doubles the bytes written, and `LIMIT -1` measuring 2.74× slower than no `LIMIT` at all).

**Lesson 29 covered savepoints and statement journals** — the one stack that holds both, and the one kind of temporary file lesson 28 did not touch. A statement transaction *is* a savepoint: `p->iStatement = db->nSavepoint + db->nStatement`, numbered above every named one. It **closed lessons 25/26's handoff by inverting its premise**: `usesStmtJournal = isMultiWrite && mayAbort`, and because nothing in a `DELETE` can raise a constraint, `mayAbort` is never set — so the entire `WHERE_ONEPASS` / `sqlite3MultiWrite()` machinery turns out to have **no effect** on statement journalling, and a multi-row `DELETE` measures `usesStmtJournal=0`. The flag is readable straight off `EXPLAIN`'s comment column on a `SQLITE_DEBUG` build, which produced a 15-row measured table: every conflict clause (`OR IGNORE`/`REPLACE`/`FAIL`/`ROLLBACK`) removes the journal, a bare `INTEGER PRIMARY KEY` is enough to require it, a `RAISE(ABORT)` trigger flips even a single-row `INSERT … VALUES`, and `PRAGMA foreign_keys=ON` changes the I/O profile of statements that never mention a foreign key. The headline measurement is the **64 KB spill threshold**, predicted in closed form (`4100 × 16 = 65,600 > 65,536`) and then hit to the single input row: re-dirtying a prefix of a 581-page table inside a savepoint kept 15 records in memory at `K=6146` and created a real file at `K=6147`, writing exactly 65,600 bytes in exactly two `xWrite`s. Below that threshold a statement journal has **zero filesystem footprint** — no `xOpen`, no name, nothing for a VFS shim to see — which is why four successive harnesses concluded statement journals do not exist. Also decoded: the sub-journal record is `4-byte big-endian pgno + raw page` with **no header and no checksum**, against the main journal's 512-byte header and per-record checksum, both captured in the same run; and a savepoint stores a *position* (`iOffset`, `iSubRec`, `nOrig`), not a snapshot, which is why rollback replays the main journal first and the sub-journal only for pages it cannot cover. Lesson 30 takes virtual tables and the `xBestIndex` protocol — where `sqlite3VtabSavepoint()`, called from every branch of `OP_Savepoint`, re-exports this stack to extensions.

## Format

Day folders live at `YYYY/MM/YYYY-MM-DD-<slug>/`:

- `README.md`: the track lesson. If a lesson is genuinely large, `README.md` becomes a short overview linking numbered sub-documents (`01-<subtopic>.md`, `02-<subtopic>.md`, …) in the same folder.
- `daily-diff.md`: that day's Daily Diff digest.

Every document starts with an `entry-meta` HTML comment (date, type, track, lesson, title, slug). The index below is generated from those blocks.

A lesson contains, in order:

1. Title and a one-line `date · track · lesson N of T` header
2. **Where This Fits**: previous lesson, what this adds, what it sets up
3. A point-wise breakdown of the real mechanics (headed sections, tables, code; no prose walls)
4. At least one structural Mermaid diagram
5. **Hands-On**: something to run that makes the internals visible, with what to look for
6. **Where This Breaks Down**: limits, costs, and tradeoffs
7. **Further Study**, 8. **Next Steps**, 9. **Sources** (only links verified that day), 10. **Takeaways**

Entries before 2026-09-16 predate the track model. They came from a five-slot topic rotation (Database Internals, System Design / Paper Analysis, Tech Blog Analysis, The Daily Diff Digest, Variety) and are kept in the index as history.
