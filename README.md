# Daily Learning Log

A daily technical learning log that goes deep. Each entry targets the actual mechanics of a system (storage layouts, wire protocols, scheduler behaviour, failure modes) rather than a feature tour or a summary of marketing pages.

Every lesson is written to be *runnable*: it carries a hands-on exercise you can execute against a real system, at least one structural diagram of the mechanism under discussion, and a source list containing only links that were actually read while writing it.

## How This Works

- **One subject at a time.** The log follows a multi-week *track*: a sequential deep dive into a single database or systems subject. The track's syllabus and progress live in [`TRACK.md`](TRACK.md), which is the single source of truth for what has been taught and what comes next.
- **One lesson per day, each building on the last.** Every day takes the first unchecked lesson from the syllabus. Lessons assume everything earlier in the track is known and open with a *Where This Fits* section linking the previous lesson. Missed days are never backfilled; the track simply continues.
- **Scope is handed forward explicitly.** A comment block in `TRACK.md` records, per lesson, what it took, what it deliberately left for a later lesson, which source commit it read, which measurements reproduced, and which did not. That is what keeps a 33-lesson sequence from re-teaching itself or quietly contradicting an earlier day. `TRACK.md` keeps the notes for the three most recent lessons; older ones roll off into git history, and [`TRACK-ARCHIVE.md`](TRACK-ARCHIVE.md) records the exact commit and blob to read for each lesson range. Nothing is deleted and nothing is duplicated — the lesson documents themselves are the real record.
- **A separate Daily Diff digest every day.** Alongside the lesson, each day folder has a `daily-diff.md` covering an edition of [The Daily Diff](https://tdd.cat/): a point-wise summary plus a deep dive on the single most significant item, with its source link followed and the underlying thing read. tdd.cat publishes editions on a documented two-day settling window, and **its endpoints are independently cached, so any one of them can be a day behind, fail outright, or omit a published edition altogether.** The front page, [`/md`](https://tdd.cat/md) (the documented "latest edition" endpoint), the dated `https://tdd.cat/{YYYY-MM-DD}.md` permalink, the dated `https://tdd.cat/{YYYY-MM-DD}/` page and [`/archive/`](https://tdd.cat/archive/) have each been observed leading, lagging or failing while the others worked, in a **different pattern on each of six consecutive days**: on 2026-10-11 the dated pages would not fetch at all and the front page was stale; on 2026-10-12 the dated page was the *only* one with the newest edition and `/md` was stale; on 2026-10-13 the front page and `/archive/` agreed and were current, `.md` worked, and the dated trailing-slash page was the one that failed; on 2026-10-14 the dated trailing-slash page failed again while the dated `.md` and `/md` both worked **and agreed on the item count**, with the front page a day behind; on **2026-10-15 the dated `.md` permalink was the *only* endpoint that served the 2026-10-14 edition at all** — `/archive/` showed that date on its calendar with **no link** and listed 2026-10-13 as newest, `/md` served 2026-10-13, the front page served 2026-10-12, RSS served 2026-10-08, and the dated trailing-slash page failed for the third day running; on **2026-10-16** the pattern inverted again: `/archive/` was current and correct, listing 2026-10-15 as newest with 55 stories, and the dated `.md` permalink agreed on the count, so the cross-check succeeded for the first time in three days — while the front page was a day behind and no 2026-10-16 edition existed at all; and on **2026-10-17 it inverted back**: the dated `.md` permalink was again the only endpoint serving the newest edition (2026-10-16, 39 items), `/archive/` listed 2026-10-15 as newest with 16 and 17 unlinked on its calendar, the front page served 2026-10-15, and `/md` served **2026-10-13** — three days behind — so no second endpoint served the covered edition and the item count could not be cross-checked. So the procedure is: take the newest date from [`/archive/`](https://tdd.cat/archive/), **then also try the next day or two forward**, fetching **both** `https://tdd.cat/{date}/` and `https://tdd.cat/{date}.md` for each, use whichever answers, and cross-check the item count against any other endpoint that answers — recording explicitly when no second endpoint serves that edition, as happened on 2026-10-15 and again on 2026-10-17. ([`llms.txt`](https://tdd.cat/llms.txt) is the source for the endpoint list.) A digest covers the newest edition not yet covered, says which one it is, and records how it was found. When no new edition has published, the digest says so explicitly rather than inventing items. Editions are identified by **date**, never by the archive's edition number, which has been observed to move *backwards* between consecutive days (079 for 2026-10-11, then 055 for 2026-10-12); item counts vary per edition and vary *wildly* — 30, 26, 18, 21, **3** and then **35** on six consecutive days, the largest immediately after the smallest, which is why the count is always cross-checked against a second endpoint when one is available before a short edition is reported as short.
- **Tracks end with a capstone.** When every lesson is checked, that day's document is a capstone assembling the whole system end to end. The subject moves to *Completed Subjects* in `TRACK.md`, and a new track begins.

**SQLite Internals**: Lesson 32 of 33 · started 2026-09-16 · [syllabus and progress &rarr;](TRACK.md)

Part I (the on-disk format and the b-tree layer), Part II (the pager, journals, and the OS interface), Part III (write-ahead logging in depth) and Part IV (the SQL compiler and bytecode engine) are complete. **Part V — operational and advanced topics — is under way.** Lesson 23 covered the VDBE itself (the single allocation holding program, registers and cursors; the bump-plus-eight-slot register allocator; the `Mem` flag word caching two types at once); lesson 24 covered the type system around that flag word (affinity as seven consecutive ASCII characters riding in `OP_Eq.p5`, and a `UNIQUE … COLLATE NOCASE` index that rejects two genuinely distinct keys); lesson 25 covered the code that emits all of it; lesson 26 covered the decision layer (`WhereLoop` as an *algorithm* carrying `(rSetup, rRun, nOut)`, the five closed-form cost formulas, the tie rule that hands equal costs to the full table scan, and the `SEARCH`/`SCAN` crossover predicted in closed form and matched to the single row at four table sizes); lesson 27 covered the statistics those costs are computed from (`sqlite_stat1`, `sqlite_stat4`'s 24 samples chosen partly by a deterministic LCG, `aAvgEq` derived to the unit, and `PRAGMA analysis_limit=100` writing `101` where the truth was `19048`); lesson 28 covered the VDBE sorter (the PMA on-disk format decoded byte by byte, the 16-PMA cliff reachable by *one page* of `cache_size`, and `LIMIT -1` measuring 2.74× slower than no `LIMIT` at all); lesson 29 covered savepoints and statement journals (a statement transaction *is* a savepoint at `db->nSavepoint + db->nStatement`; the `WHERE_ONEPASS` machinery shown to have **no effect** on statement journalling because `mayAbort` is never set for a `DELETE`; and the 64 KB spill threshold predicted in closed form and then hit to the single input row, 65,600 bytes in exactly two `xWrite`s).

**Lesson 30 covered virtual tables and the `xBestIndex` protocol**, read as the planner interface it actually is rather than as an extension tutorial. A virtual table does not get a planner — it gets one callback that fills in lesson 26's `rRun` and `nOut` on a `WhereLoop`, with `rSetup` **hard-wired to 0**, so the interesting content is what cannot be expressed through it. The headline measurement is the **width of the cost channel**: `EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN`'s third column *is* the loop's `rRun`, and sweeping one module's `estimatedCost` through it gives 1→0, 2→10, 10→33, 100→66, 1e6→199 — all exactly 10·log₂ — and then **1e12→400 where 10·log₂ is 399, and the default 5e98→3280 where it is 3279**, because above 2×10⁹ `sqlite3LogEstFromDouble` stops computing a logarithm and reads the IEEE-754 exponent field (`e = (a>>52)-1022; return e*10`), quantising cost to one value per power of two. The documented "very large" default cost is exactly **5e98** (`SQLITE_BIG_DBL/2`), read from inside the callback along with `estimatedRows = 25`, and both are reset on *every* call — because `xBestIndex` runs up to **K+4 times** per table per query, measured at 1 call for `WHERE a=5` (the `mBest==0 && !bIn` short circuit), 2 for a join, and 2 for `a IN (1,2,3)`, which also produced **three** `xFilter` calls since `WO_IN` is folded to `SQLITE_INDEX_CONSTRAINT_EQ` and there is no `..._IN`. Also measured: a module with a good `xCreate` and a NULL `xDestroy` is registered and works eponymously but fails `CREATE VIRTUAL TABLE` with the misleading `no such module`, and `sqlite3_create_module` returns `SQLITE_OK` either way; `DIRECTONLY` is enforced at *use*, so `CREATE VIEW v AS SELECT * FROM dbstat` succeeds and reading `v` then fails; `dbstat`'s `idxNum` bitmask is legible straight off the plan text in hex (`0x2` for `name=?`, `0xa` adding `orderByConsumed`) only because it sets `SQLITE_INDEX_SCAN_HEX`; and `dbstat` and `sqlite_dbpage` agree, byte for byte, that leaf page 4's `8 + 2·30 + (4096−85) = 4079` leaves exactly the **17 unused bytes** dbstat reports — lesson 03 and lesson 04's arithmetic confirmed from two independent modules. The real find **closes lesson 29's `sqlite3VtabSavepoint` handoff and contradicts the documentation**: `sqlite_dbpage`'s entire truncation mechanism is one `Pgno` field, which explains three of its four documented limitations mechanically, and the fourth — "any `ROLLBACK TO` will cancel the null-INSERT" — **is false as stated**, because `xRollbackTo` is skipped unless `pVTab->iSavepoint > iSavepoint` and that is only set for vtabs already in `db->aVTrans[]` when the savepoint was taken. Two statement sequences differing *only* in whether a page had been written before `SAVEPOINT s1` truncate to 99 and 120 pages respectively. Lesson 31 took memory-mapped I/O and memory allocation.

**Lesson 31 covered memory-mapped I/O and the allocator stack** — the three places a byte can live in a connection, and how narrow the conditions are for getting the free one. `PRAGMA mmap_size` maps a **prefix** of the file, never a set of hot pages: on a 15,712,256-byte database whose index lives at the tail, `mmap_size` of 256 KB, 1 MB and 4 MB each produced **581 `xFetch` calls and zero mappings** while still reading all 582 pages through `xRead`. The gate is `unixFetch`'s **256-byte end-of-file buffer** — `mmapSize >= iOff + nAmt + 256` — which means the mapping can never reach the last page of any database, and which moved exactly one page across the line per 256 bytes of `mmap_size`, predicted in closed form and confirmed in both directions. Where it does apply the win is memory, not time: the same scan used **146,864 bytes of heap against 2,166,280** (14.8×) with `CACHE_MISS` falling from 582 to 2, because a mapped page is a `PgHdr` recycled through `pPager->pMmapFreelist` and never enters the page cache — while wall clock moved only 6.4 → 5.6 ms on a host-cached file. A read inside a write transaction is still mapped (`PAGER_GET_READONLY`), an `UPDATE` issued **zero** `xFetch` calls, and in WAL mode 182 of 581 pages dropped out of the mappable set after a 200-row update, to be restored by checkpointing. **Lesson 28's open sorter-mmap question is closed, and all three of its candidate causes were wrong**: `db->nMaxSorterMmap` is only initialised under `SQLITE_ENABLE_SORTER_MMAP`, so by default no `xFetch` is issued at all; and with the feature enabled the trace shows `mmapSizeMax` at the full `SQLITE_MAX_MMAP_SIZE` and a mapping built exactly as asked, failing only because `vdbeSorterMapFile` requests the whole file at offset 0 and the preallocation overshoots by **9 bytes** where 256 are required — a final gap of **250 bytes**. On the allocator side: a cached 4,096-byte page costs **4,368 bytes** and the first twenty arrive in one 87,360-byte bulk allocation; memsys5 costs **1.81×** the memory for the same scan because 4,368 rounds to 8,192, and no legal page size avoids it; and the soft heap limit is honoured within 1.2% **even in a build where `sqlite3_release_memory` is literally `return 0`**, because the mechanism is `pcache1UnderMemoryPressure` making the cache recycle rather than grow — the tell being `CACHE_MISS` unchanged at 582 across every limit. Finally, `setupLookaside`'s three-branch arithmetic was predicted in closed form and matched at **nine configurations in a row**: the default `1200,40` is a 48,000-byte budget that yields **123 slots** (30 large + 93 small), `1200,100` yields 309, `256,1000` yields 1334 and `248,1000` yields 1000 — eight bytes of `sz` crossing a branch boundary — and `sz=8` silently disables the feature entirely. It buys **1.31×** on prepares and is exhausted permanently by an application holding a few hundred statements open. Lesson 32 took the verifier itself.

**Lesson 32 covered `PRAGMA integrity_check` and SQLite's test strategy** — the verifier, read as the oracle the whole project is tested against rather than as a maintenance command. It is **two checkers, not one**: a C b-tree walk (`sqlite3BtreeIntegrityCheck`) with exactly **two heap allocations** — a **282-byte** page-reachability bitmap on the 2,254-page test database and a **4,096-byte min-heap** — and a **generated VDBE program** that scans every table with every index cursor open. The min-heap is the elegant part: each cell and freeblock becomes `(start<<16)|end`, an **implied first entry at `contentOffset - 1`** covers the header and cell pointer array, and pulling in order proves in one pass that every byte of the page is used **exactly once** while the gaps sum to the fragmented-bytes field — compared only when the heap drained cleanly, so one fault yields one message. `aRoot` for `main` on a two-object schema is `[2,3,1]`: **page 1 is checked as an ordinary table b-tree**, because `sqlite_schema` is an entry in the schema's table hash. `checkTreePage` **zeroes `pPage->isInit`** before `btreeInitPage` so an already-validated cached page is re-validated, which is the difference between a checker and a cache tour and is reachable — a page type byte of `0x07` gives exactly `btreeInitPage() returns error code 11`. The headline correction is to `quick_check`: there is **one consequential `if( !isQuick )` block**, so `quick_check` still parses every record header, still compares **index entry counts** (partial indexes excluded), and still enforces `NOT NULL`, STRICT/affinity types, `CHECK` constraints and `xIntegrity`. What it skips is the per-row index probe and `UNIQUE` — and that is the gap that matters: **one flipped byte inside an index key, and a pair of index entries swapped so the keys are out of order, are both `ok` to `quick_check` and both fatal to `integrity_check`**, because the C walk's ordering test is guarded by `if( pPage->intKey )` and never checks index key order at all. Cost was measured two ways and the answer is cache, not asymptotics: with a cache holding the whole file both pragmas issue **2,254 `xRead` calls — exactly the page count** — at 19.8 ms versus 94.1 ms (4.8× CPU, 1.0× I/O), while at the default `cache_size` `integrity_check` issues **35,380 reads, 15.7 per page of the file**. Scaling across 25k→400k rows gave `quick_check` four consecutive factors of 1.92–2.08 and `integrity_check` ≈2.1×, so the documented O(N log N) is real but worth only 5–10% per doubling against a 4.8× constant. Also measured: the error cap **truncates the walk** (1,619 versus 2,258 pages read), `database disk image is malformed` arrives as a **result row** because the pragma stores an escape address in **P3 of instruction 0** that `op_column_corrupt` jumps to, a partial `integrity_check(t)` is **weaker without being faster** (125.8 ms versus 121.1 ms) while `integrity_check(sqlite_schema)` costs 1 read and still validates the freelist, and the check aborts **mid-walk** on `sqlite3_interrupt` at 18 ms into 123 ms. **Lesson 31's `xIntegrity` handoff is closed**: `mFlags` is exactly `isQuick`, a non-NULL `*pzErr` with `SQLITE_OK` is a finding rather than an error, and an **eponymous virtual table is never checked** — the pass walks the schema's table hash, which `dbstat`, `sqlite_dbpage` and `pragma_*` are not in. Against ~155.8 KSLOC of library there are ~92 MSLOC of tests (**≈590:1**), and `integrity_check` is the assertion in all three anomaly harnesses: after simulated I/O errors, after TCL's random-point crashes, and after TH3's snapshot damage patterns. Lesson 33 takes `VACUUM` and `VACUUM INTO`.

## Format

Day folders live at `YYYY/MM/YYYY-MM-DD-<slug>/`:

- `README.md`: the track lesson. If a lesson is genuinely large, `README.md` becomes a short overview linking numbered sub-documents (`01-<subtopic>.md`, `02-<subtopic>.md`, …) in the same folder.
- `daily-diff.md`: that day's Daily Diff digest.

Every document starts with an `entry-meta` HTML comment (date, type, track, lesson, title, slug). The index below is generated from those blocks.

A lesson contains, in order:

1. Title and a one-line `date · track · lesson N of T` header
2. **Where This Fits**: previous lesson, what this adds, what it sets up
3. A point-wise breakdown of the real mechanics (headed sections, tables, code; no prose walls)
4. At least one structural Mermaid diagram
5. **Hands-On**: something to run that makes the internals visible, with what to look for
6. **Where This Breaks Down**: limits, costs, and tradeoffs
7. **Further Study**, 8. **Next Steps**, 9. **Sources** (only links verified that day), 10. **Takeaways**

Entries before 2026-09-16 predate the track model. They came from a five-slot topic rotation (Database Internals, System Design / Paper Analysis, Tech Blog Analysis, The Daily Diff Digest, Variety) and are kept in the index as history.
