# Daily Learning Log

A daily technical learning log that goes deep. Each entry targets the actual mechanics of a system (storage layouts, wire protocols, scheduler behaviour, failure modes) rather than a feature tour or a summary of marketing pages.

Every lesson is written to be *runnable*: it carries a hands-on exercise you can execute against a real system, at least one structural diagram of the mechanism under discussion, and a source list containing only links that were actually read while writing it.

## How This Works

- **One subject at a time.** The log follows a multi-week *track*: a sequential deep dive into a single database or systems subject. The track's syllabus and progress live in [`TRACK.md`](TRACK.md), which is the single source of truth for what has been taught and what comes next. For a long track, the append-only registers — PR candidates found along the way, and the ledger of open questions with the lesson that owns each — live beside it under [`tracks/`](tracks/); the JanusGraph track's are in [`tracks/janusgraph-registers.md`](tracks/janusgraph-registers.md).
- **One lesson per day, each building on the last.** Every day takes the first unchecked lesson from the syllabus. Lessons assume everything earlier in the track is known and open with a *Where This Fits* section linking the previous lesson. Missed days are never backfilled; the track simply continues.
- **Scope is handed forward explicitly.** A comment block in `TRACK.md` records, per lesson, what it took, what it deliberately left for a later lesson, which source commit it read, which measurements reproduced, and which did not. That is what keeps a long sequence from re-teaching itself or quietly contradicting an earlier day. `TRACK.md` keeps the notes for the three most recent lessons; older ones roll off into git history, and [`TRACK-ARCHIVE.md`](TRACK-ARCHIVE.md) records the exact commit and blob to read for each lesson range. Nothing is deleted and nothing is duplicated — the lesson documents themselves are the real record.

- **A separate Daily Diff digest every day.** Alongside the lesson, each day folder has a `daily-diff.md` covering an edition of [The Daily Diff](https://tdd.cat/): a point-wise summary plus a deep dive on the single most significant item, with its source link followed and the underlying thing read.   **tdd.cat's endpoints are independently cached and disagree with each other, and thirty consecutive runs produced thirty different lead/lag patterns.** The front page, [`/md`](https://tdd.cat/md), the dated `https://tdd.cat/{YYYY-MM-DD}.md` permalink, the dated `https://tdd.cat/{YYYY-MM-DD}/` page, [`/archive/`](https://tdd.cat/archive/), [RSS](https://tdd.cat/rss.xml) and the per-edition PDF have each been observed leading, lagging, failing or disagreeing while the others worked. **The procedure, as it stands after the 2026-11-16 amendment:**

  1. Fetch the front page and [`/archive/`](https://tdd.cat/archive/) and take the **maximum** date either names. A *larger* archive maximum is a reason to probe that date; a *smaller* one is no reason to doubt the front page (2026-11-05).
  2. **Then probe `https://tdd.cat/{today}/` and `https://tdd.cat/{yesterday}/` directly**, because a published edition can be absent from *both* indexes while they agree with each other (2026-11-13, and again 2026-11-15). Try the no-slash form too — the trailing slash is part of the cache key and the two forms have disagreed in one run (2026-10-28).
  3. **Check the `TRACK.md` session log in both directions** before concluding anything: a floor, so a run does not re-digest the edition the previous entry covered (2026-11-07), and a ceiling, so it does not skip an uncovered one by invoking the fallback mechanically (2026-11-09).
  4. **Fallback, last resort only:** if the newest available edition is one a previous entry already digested and no newer edition exists, state the gap with timestamps and endpoints checked, then go deep on **one item from that edition the previous digest did not cover**, saying in the document that that is what is happening (2026-11-08).
  5. Identify editions by **date**, never by the archive's edition number, which has been observed moving backwards. Take the item count from the endpoint you took the items from and say which one that was — the HTML and PDF renderings of one edition have disagreed 43 against 33 (2026-10-24). Edition size carries no information about completeness (2026-11-10). A large edition's page can exceed the ~100 KB one WebFetch returns, so its tail needs a second call with an offset (2026-11-11, used for real on 2026-11-13).
  6. **If the best item in an edition cannot be verified, pick another and say why** (2026-11-15). XRAID was the most on-topic item in the 2026-11-14 edition and both of its primary sources — the mailing-list archive and the author's slides — are robots-disallowed, with the secondary report stating outright that it does not describe the mechanism. A digest says that and deep-dives something it could actually read, rather than filling the gap with plausible generalities.

- **Tracks end with a capstone.** When every lesson is checked, that day's document is a capstone assembling the whole system end to end. The subject moves to *Completed Subjects* in `TRACK.md`, and a new track begins.
- **Some tracks are reading sessions, not lessons.** A track whose `mode` is `contributor-reading` targets a codebase the reader intends to contribute to. Those entries do not explain the code; they point at the exact files to open at a pinned commit, give the minimum map needed to read them efficiently, pose questions whose answers are in the code, and list live upstream issues the reading touches. The other half of each entry is a **`## My Notes`** section written by the reader, and the next session opens by answering the open questions left there. An entry whose `My Notes` stays empty did not count, and the next run says so. **A track can change mode mid-flight**, and the JanusGraph track did on 2026-11-03: a reading-session track whose `My Notes` stays empty for several consecutive sessions is generating guides nobody reads, so the mode flips to `lesson` and the entries start answering their own questions. **What the track has established so far.** The per-lesson narrative for **lessons 01–17** was rolled off this file on **2026-11-17**, under the same convention `TRACK.md` uses and that the Daily Diff observation log above was rolled off by: it had reached 32 KB in one section and the full text is preserved in this file's **git history** — the last commit carrying it in full is the one immediately preceding the 2026-11-17 entry — while the lesson documents in the index below remain the real record. In one paragraph: **sessions 01–07 established the storage contract.** A vertex is a *row*, its incident edges and properties are that row's *cells sorted by column*, and each edge is stored twice, once per endpoint row — so a backend's maximum cells per row is the maximum vertex degree JanusGraph supports on it. The column interval is half-open; a `mutate` applies deletions strictly before additions; a lock taken with `acquireLock` is only verified on the transaction's first `mutate`. The 24 `StoreFeatures` booleans a backend declares about itself are enforced by **one** `checkArgument` in the whole tree, and `Backend` reads `locking` *inverted*. The in-memory store keeps a row as two arrays — an `int[]` offset index over one packed `byte[]` — so there are no `Entry` objects at rest and a slice is two binary searches; BerkeleyJE has no column dimension at all and `OrderedKeyValueStoreAdapter` manufactures one by concatenating the row key onto every column, which is exact only for fixed-width keys. The conformance suites ship from `src/main/java` and a backend has four inequivalent ways to switch an inherited test off, one of which — `@Test` with an empty body — *passes and is counted as coverage*. **Lessons 08–13 established graph ⇄ bytes.** The vertex id Gremlin returns and the row key the backend shards on are different 64-bit words, and one line of `getKey` rotates the partition hard against the top of the key; `cluster.max-partitions` defaults to 32, so every real graph runs at 5 partition bits. Ids are allocated from in-memory blocks claimed by a write-wait-read consensus over one row of `janusgraph_ids` that runs once per 10,000 vertices, and the default 4 conflict-avoidance bits — with a tag value of `0` and mode `NONE` — make every assigned count a multiple of 16 and cost one byte in every cell of the database. `EdgeSerializer.writeRelation` writes one buffer and returns one integer, `valuePosition`, and **the column is the uniqueness constraint**: a `MANY2ONE` edge or `SINGLE` property gets a column of nothing but the type prefix, so a second write overwrites with no read and no lock. Everything after the sort keys is written backward so a parse can walk in from the tail with no length headers; a descending vertex-centric index is a one's complement over exactly the sort-key window. There is no type catalog — a property key is a vertex in the same `edgestore`, described by six system property keys and two system edge labels whose ids are computed rather than stored. **Lessons 14–17 established the write path.** `GraphDatabaseConfiguration` merges a properties file with *one row* of `system_properties` keyed by the UTF-8 bytes of the word `configuration`; every data write funnels through `connectRelation` and fans out to three destinations; `prepareCommit` is seven phases over three accumulators; and the composite-index write path makes two byte-layout decisions whose only job is to stop you doing what the storage layer would otherwise allow — a row key that cannot be range-scanned, and a column width that *is* the uniqueness level. Lessons 18 and 19 keep their full paragraphs below.

Lesson 18 closed Part III by opening `acquireLock` itself, and the answer to *how uniqueness survives an eventually consistent store* turns out to be **one boolean**. `ExpectedValueCheckingStoreManager`'s constructor rebuilds the backing manager's features as `new StandardStoreFeatures.Builder(storeManager.getFeatures()).locking(true).build()`, so every `hasLocking()` branch lesson 03 mapped sees **`true`** on a store that has no native locking at all — the layer above is never told the store cannot lock, because by the time it asks, it can. `Backend` makes that decision in four lines and nowhere else, which also means `edgestore`, `graphindex` **and** `system_properties` are each wrapped and each get their own derived `<name>_lock_` store; there are exactly **two** lock backends in the tree, `consistentkey` and `test`. Then: **`acquireLock` checks nothing.** It writes a claim row and files the expected value away; the read-and-compare happens once, on the transaction's first `mutate`, for every lock at once — which is lesson 02's promise finally spent, and the reason lesson 16's phases are ordered as they are, since a lock requested after the first write is a `PermanentLockingException` forever. Lesson 17's idempotence claim is confirmed and is implemented **twice** — an early return in `AbstractLocker.writeLock` and a `containsKey` check that keeps the **first** expected value — and that second mechanism is what makes lesson 16's *"deletions first"* load-bearing rather than cosmetic: reverse the two passes and every hand-off of a unique value from one element to another fails, because the remembered expected value would be `null` against a cell that exists. The on-disk lock row is exact: **4-byte big-endian key length ‖ key ‖ column** for the row, **8-byte timestamp ‖ rid** for the column, four zero bytes for the value, sliced from one `0x00` byte to **nine** `0xFF` bytes — and the 27-line comment justifying the nine is an argument about **UTF-8**, not about clocks, since the ninth byte is the first byte of the rid and `0xFF` is not a legal UTF-8 byte. Two of that comment's own claims are wrong against the default configuration (it says nanoseconds, the provider defaults to microseconds; it says a single zero byte, it is four). The cost is countable: for N locked `(key, column)` pairs, **2N + 1 extra backend transactions, 2N extra round trips, and a ~100 ms floor** on commit latency, because every locking transaction sleeps out `storage.lock.wait-time` before it may read its own claim back — and one lock promotes **every** write in that commit to the key-consistent transaction. Severity is inverted where you would least want it: two threads contending in the *same JVM* get a non-retryable `PermanentLockingException("Local lock contention")`, while two *different JVMs* get a temporary one and are retried. And the practical discovery, which changes what every later exercise can do cheaply: **`InMemoryStoreManager` never calls `.locking(...)`**, so the in-memory backend runs the entire `ConsistentKeyLocker` protocol — lock rows, 100 ms sleeps, expected-value re-reads — in a store that does not even persist. `InMemoryGraphTest` has been exercising the locking layer since lesson 01 without anyone noticing, and **BerkeleyJE, which lesson 17 proposed as the reproduction backend for its conjecture, never runs that code at all.**

Lesson 19 opened Part IV at the bottom of the read path and found that **the thing the documentation calls "invalidation" is a mark, not an eviction, and its release is a counter rather than a clock.** A read passes through four tiers: the per-transaction `CaffeineVertexCache` that guarantees one vertex object per id; that vertex's own `queryCache`, a `HashMap<SliceQuery, EntryList>` of size 4 with a linear `subsumes` scan behind it; the per-graph `ExpirationKCVSCache`; and the backend. `StandardVertex` is the **no-cache base** — its flat `hasLoadedRelations() == false` is the honest answer to *"do I hold this `SliceQuery`'s result?"*, not a stub — and `VertexConstructor` makes **every user vertex in an ordinary transaction a `CacheVertex`**, reserving `PreloadedVertex` for OLAP. Tier 1 saves the *read* and not the *decode*: every tier stores raw `EntryList`s, so a cache hit still re-runs lesson 11's `parseRelation` per entry. **The finding is what a commit does to tier 2.** `CacheTransaction.flushInternal` persists first and then calls `cache.invalidate(key, EMPTY_LIST)` per mutated row key; `ExpirationKCVSCache.invalidate` writes `expiredKeys[key] = now + db-cache-time` and nothing is evicted. While that mark stands the row is not merely uncached but **uncacheable** — `isExpired` is true, the read goes to the store and the result is deliberately **not** put back, on all three read paths, enforced by a `key -> null` sentinel rather than by a second expiry check. Release requires `PENALTY_THRESHOLD = 5` countdowns of a `CountDownLatch` (each expired read counts one; each `invalidate` counts one with probability **1/1000**) **and** an age of at least `db-cache-clean-wait`. That is not a reconstruction: `ExpirationCacheTest.testGracePeriod`'s local `minCleanupTriggerCalls = 5` is `PENALTY_THRESHOLD` verbatim, and the loop it sits in does nothing but count the latch down — which is also why that test and its sibling are both flaky with their own issue numbers. The corollary inverts the documentation's advice: **`cache.db-cache-time = 0`, recommended for a single instance, makes the invalidation mark eternal too**, because `getExpirationTime()` uses the same `cacheTimeMS`, so the self-healing branch can never fire and release falls entirely to the probabilistic cleanup. A second structural finding kills a branch: **`hasValidateKeysOnly()` returns `true` and is overridden nowhere in the repository** — three `search_code` hits, all accounted for — so the `else` arm of `flushInternal`, the one that builds a `List<CachableStaticBuffer>` of per-column entries, is unreachable, and column-level invalidation is scaffolding rather than code. Smaller things worth carrying: *"modified vertices are pinned in the cache"* is implemented **reactively**, by a Caffeine removal listener that rescues `isModified() || isRemoved()` vertices into an unbounded `volatileVertices`, so `cache.tx-cache-size` bounds clean vertices only; `indexCacheWeight` is not an option at all but `tx-cache-size / 2`, which is where the docs' 9,998-element ceiling comes from; `readOnlyOLAP()` sets the tier-0 cache to `maximumSize(0)` because the 100-element floor is skipped for read-only transactions; `refresh()` has exactly one production caller chain, `ManagementSystem`/`ManagementLogger` &rarr; `tx.expireSchemaElement` &rarr; `sv.refresh()`, so a user-data vertex is never refreshed by JanusGraph itself; and the public two-argument `addToQueryCache` **skips** the refresh-counter guard the three-argument one exists to enforce, on the graph-index read path. ## Format

Day folders live at `YYYY/MM/YYYY-MM-DD-<slug>/`:

- `README.md`: the track entry — a lesson, or, in a `contributor-reading` track, a reading session. If a lesson is genuinely large, `README.md` becomes a short overview linking numbered sub-documents (`01-<subtopic>.md`, `02-<subtopic>.md`, …) in the same folder; reading sessions are never split.
- `daily-diff.md`: that day's Daily Diff digest.

Every document starts with an `entry-meta` HTML comment (date, type, track, lesson, title, slug). The index below is generated from those blocks. `type` is `lesson`, `reading-session`, `daily-diff`, or `capstone`; a capstone carries `lesson: —` and shows `—` in the `#` column of the index.

A lesson contains, in order:

1. Title and a one-line `date · track · lesson N of T` header
2. **Where This Fits**: previous lesson, what this adds, what it sets up
3. A point-wise breakdown of the real mechanics (headed sections, tables, code; no prose walls)
4. At least one structural Mermaid diagram
5. **Hands-On**: something to run that makes the internals visible, with what to look for
6. **Where This Breaks Down**: limits, costs, and tradeoffs
7. **Further Study**, 8. **Next Steps**, 9. **Sources** (only links verified that day), 10. **Takeaways**

A reading session contains instead: the header line with the pinned short SHA; **From Your Last Notes** (answers to the previous session's open questions, skipped on session 01); **Where This Fits**; **Today's Reading** (the files to open as pinned permalinks, with line ranges and what to skip); **Map**; **Questions To Answer From The Code**; **Hands-On**; **Contribution Radar** (real open upstream issues); **My Notes** (the reader's half); **Sources**. It carries no *Where This Breaks Down*, *Further Study*, *Next Steps* or *Takeaways* — those would be the entry doing the reader's thinking for him.

Entries before 2026-09-16 predate the track model. They came from a five-slot topic rotation (Database Internals, System Design / Paper Analysis, Tech Blog Analysis, The Daily Diff Digest, Variety) and are kept in the index as history.
